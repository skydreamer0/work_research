## Why this source matters
- This bundle was assembled to see whether Slide 9 can discuss practicality and patient acceptance without making efficacy claims.

## Source excerpt or extracted evidence
- Comparative studies support that dosage form can influence acceptability and patient preference.
- Survey and claims evidence support convenience, satisfaction, persistence, and adherence themes more than efficacy differentiation.
- A systematic review suggests major efficacy differences between vaginal estrogen dosage forms should not be the main message.
- The safest formulation slide focuses on practical use and patient acceptance, not superior efficacy.

## Key quantitative details
- Population: Postmenopausal women using local vaginal estrogen
- Intervention / exposure: Ring, pessary, cream, or tablet depending on the source
- Comparator: Alternate dosage forms and real-world persistence comparisons
- Endpoint: Preference, acceptance, ease of use, persistence, adherence
- Effect size / estimate: Narrative rather than one pooled numeric effect selected for slide use
- P value / CI / OR / RR / HR / ICER: Not consolidated for slide use

## Evidence quality notes
- Study design: Mixed comparative trial, survey, claims, and review evidence
- Major strengths: Supports cautious acceptance and persistence language
- Major weaknesses: Some evidence is older, non-randomized, or subject to self-selection; not ideal for superiority claims

## Communication risk notes
- Approved use relevance: Good for practical internal training
- Off-label risk: Low if kept focused on patient experience and persistence
- Overclaim risk: Medium if generalized into a product efficacy advantage
- Comparative claim risk: Medium
- Overall communication class: Education-safe
